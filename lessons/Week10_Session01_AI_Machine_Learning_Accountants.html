AI fundamentals

What AI is and is not

SCRIPT: "First, let's clear up definitions. AI is not magic. It is not sentient. It is pattern recognition at scale."

How ML Works

The learning process (simplified)

  • 1. Data: Collect historical examples (invoices, transactions, fraud cases)
  • 2. Training: Algorithm finds patterns in the data
  • 3. Model: The learned patterns become a model (like a recipe)
  • 4. Prediction: New data is run through the model to get an answer
  • 5. Feedback: Correct predictions are confirmed, errors improve the model

Key insight: ML learns from HISTORICAL data. If the history is biased, incomplete, or wrong, the model will be too. "Garbage in, garbage out" still applies.

SCRIPT: "The critical thing to understand: ML is NOT programmed with rules. It discovers patterns from data. That means if your training data has bias — for example, historical lending decisions that discriminated — the model will LEARN that discrimination. This is why AI ethics matters."
AI vs Rules

When to use AI vs. traditional rules

Use rules when:

• Logic is clear and stable
• Regulations define exact thresholds
• Auditability requires explicit reasoning
• Volume is low and context is simple
• Example: "If overdue > 90 days, classify as doubtful"

Use AI when:

• Patterns are complex and not easily coded
• Data volume is high and varied
• Human judgment is slow or inconsistent
• Predictions from patterns add value
• Example: "Which invoices are likely to be paid late?"

SCRIPT: "Not everything needs AI. If a rule is clear and stable, a simple IF-THEN is better: cheaper, explainable, auditable. Use AI when patterns are too complex for manual rules, when you have lots of data, or when you need predictions. Both can coexist."

AI in accounting

Practical applications that exist today

SCRIPT: "Let's get specific. Where is AI already used in accounting? Not future dreams — CURRENT tools that firms use today."
Applications

AI applications in accounting (today)

Document processing

OCR + AI reads invoices, receipts, contracts. Extracts fields automatically.

Anomaly detection

Flags unusual transactions for auditors. Detects potential fraud patterns.

Cash flow forecasting

Predicts future cash position based on historical patterns and receivables.

Audit analytics

Tests 100% of transactions instead of sampling. Identifies risk areas.

Tax research

AI searches regulations, interprets scenarios, suggests treatments.

Report generation

Drafts narratives, management discussion, variance explanations.

SCRIPT: "These are REAL applications in use today. Big 4 firms use AI for audit analytics. Cloud accounting tools use AI for receipt scanning. Banks use AI for fraud detection. Tax software uses AI for research assistance. You will encounter these in your first job."
Case: Intelligent Document Processing

How AI reads invoices

  • Input: Scanned PDF or photo of a supplier invoice
  • OCR layer: Converts image to machine-readable text
  • AI extraction: Identifies vendor name, date, amount, line items, tax
  • Validation: Checks against vendor master, duplicate detection, format rules
  • Human review: Staff confirms or corrects extracted data
  • Learning: Corrections improve future accuracy

Tools: Rossum, Kofax, ABBYY, Microsoft AI Builder, Xero's Hubdoc

SCRIPT: "This is one of the most mature AI accounting applications. Instead of manually typing invoice data, AI reads it. But notice: HUMAN REVIEW remains. AI is not 100% accurate, especially with messy documents. The accountant reviews and corrects. Over time, the AI improves."

Generative AI for Accountants

How GenAI assists (not replaces) accounting work

Current uses

• Draft management discussion and analysis
• Research tax regulations and summarize
• Generate variance explanations
• Create report templates and checklists
• Translate technical standards to plain language

Critical rules

• NEVER trust without verification
• Never input confidential client data into public AI
• Always disclose AI assistance where required
• The accountant owns the final output
• AI is a draft tool, not an authority

SCRIPT: "GenAI like ChatGPT or Claude can DRAFT content, but YOU verify, edit, and own the final product. Never paste client financial data into public AI tools. Never present AI output as your own analysis without review. Use it as a research assistant, not a decision-maker."

AI risks and ethics

What can go wrong and how to govern AI responsibly

SCRIPT: "AI is powerful but comes with serious risks. Understanding these risks is what separates a responsible professional from someone who blindly trusts technology."
AI Risks

Six risks every accountant must know

Hallucination

AI generates confident but FALSE information. Cites fake sources, invents data.

Bias

AI reflects biases in training data. Can discriminate in lending, hiring, auditing.

Privacy breach

Confidential data entered into AI may be stored, used for training, or leaked.

Overreliance

Trusting AI without verification. Automation complacency. Skill atrophy.

Lack of explainability

Cannot explain WHY the AI made a decision. "Black box" problem.

Accountability gap

When AI makes an error, who is responsible? The user? Vendor? Developer?

SCRIPT: "These are not hypothetical. AI hallucinations happen DAILY — ChatGPT has cited fake court cases. Bias in lending algorithms has been documented. Data breaches through AI tools have occurred. Overreliance leads to errors going undetected. As professionals, WE remain accountable for outputs that use AI."
AI Ethics Framework

Responsible AI principles for accounting

  • Transparency: Disclose where AI is used in your work
  • Accountability: A human is always responsible for the final decision
  • Fairness: Check for bias in AI-generated analyses and recommendations
  • Privacy: Protect client data — never feed confidential information to public AI
  • Accuracy: Verify all AI outputs against authoritative sources
  • Explainability: Be able to explain your reasoning, not just "the AI said so"
SCRIPT: "These six principles should guide any AI adoption in your practice. Can you explain it? Can you verify it? Is it fair? Is data protected? Is someone accountable? If the answer to ANY is 'no,' reconsider the approach."

Decision Framework

Should we use AI for this task?

  • 1. Is the problem suitable? (Pattern-based, high volume, data available)
  • 2. Is the risk acceptable? (Error impact, bias risk, privacy exposure)
  • 3. Can we verify outputs? (Reconciliation, human review, audit trail)
  • 4. Can we explain the result? (To clients, auditors, regulators)
  • 5. Do we have governance? (Policy, training, monitoring)
  • If all yes → proceed with controls. If any no → address the gap first.
SCRIPT: "Use this five-question test before adopting AI for any accounting task. If you can't verify outputs, don't deploy it. If you can't explain it, don't use it for client-facing work. Professional judgment applies."
Activity

AI opportunity assessment

  • In groups, choose one accounting process (audit, tax, reporting, bookkeeping)
  • Identify ONE specific task where AI could help
  • Apply the 5-question decision framework
  • List: benefit, risk, required controls, human review points
  • Present a 2-minute recommendation: adopt, pilot, or reject
SCRIPT: "Apply the framework to a real process. Don't just say 'AI can help everything.' Be specific about the task, the benefit, the risk, and the controls. Show professional judgment."
Recap

AI for accountants: key lessons

  • AI = pattern recognition at scale. ML learns from data. GenAI creates content.
  • Use rules for clear logic. Use AI for complex patterns and predictions.
  • Current applications: document processing, anomaly detection, forecasting, drafting
  • Six risks: hallucination, bias, privacy, overreliance, explainability, accountability
  • Always verify, always disclose, always have a human accountable
  • AI won't replace accountants. Accountants WHO USE AI will replace those who don't.
SCRIPT: "That last point is the most important. AI is a tool. Like Excel transformed accounting in the 1990s, AI is transforming it now. Those who learn to use it responsibly will thrive. Those who ignore it will fall behind. Those who trust it blindly will make serious mistakes. The middle path: informed, responsible adoption."
