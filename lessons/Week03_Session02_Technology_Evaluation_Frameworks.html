SCRIPT: "The Weighted Scoring Model — most practical framework. How it works: First, identify your criteria — what matters to you? Functionality, cost, ease of use, integration, support, security. Second, ASSIGN WEIGHTS — what's MOST important? If cost is king, give it 30%. If functionality matters most, weight it higher. Third, score each option 1-5 for each criterion. Framework 2

Total Cost of Ownership (TCO) Analysis

Upfront Costs

• License/subscription fees
• Hardware purchases
• Implementation/setup
• Data migration
• Initial training

Ongoing Costs

• Monthly/annual subscription
• Maintenance & updates
• Support fees
• Additional user licenses
• Internet/bandwidth

Hidden Costs

• Productivity loss during transition
• Customization needs
• Integration development
• Staff turnover/retraining
• Exit/migration costs later

Rule of Thumb: The purchase price is only 20-30% of Total Cost of Ownership. Hidden and ongoing costs are 70-80%!

SCRIPT: "TCO — Total Cost of Ownership. Biggest mistake organizations make: looking only at the purchase price. 'Option A is ₱500/month, Option B is ₱1,500/month — obviously A!' WRONG. You must consider ALL costs. UPFRONT: setup, migration, training. ONGOING: monthly fees, maintenance, support. HIDDEN: productivity loss during transition (employees learn new system = slower work for weeks), customization, integration costs, ug exit costs if you want to switch later. The purchase price? Only 20-30% of true cost! A 'cheap' system with bad support, complex setup, ug constant customization needs ends up MORE expensive. Always do TCO analysis."
Framework 3

SWOT Analysis for Technology Options

Strengths (Internal +)

What does this technology do WELL? Best features, proven reliability, strong vendor

Weaknesses (Internal -)

What are its LIMITATIONS? Missing features, complexity, cost, learning curve

Opportunities (External +)

What can we GAIN? Competitive advantage, efficiency, integration possibilities, market growth

Threats (External -)

What could go WRONG? Vendor shutdown, security risks, regulatory changes, obsolescence

SCRIPT: "SWOT Analysis — familiar na mo ani from other courses, pero we apply it to TECHNOLOGY evaluation. Strengths: what's this technology good at? Weaknesses: what's it bad at? What's missing? Opportunities: what could we GAIN by adopting it? Threats: what external risks exist? Vendor might go bankrupt? Technology might become obsolete? New regulations might make it non-compliant? SWOT is great for QUALITATIVE assessment — it captures things that numbers can't. Use it alongside the Weighted Scoring Model for a complete picture."
Framework 4

Fit-Gap Analysis

Compare your REQUIREMENTS against what the technology PROVIDES.

SCRIPT: "Fit-Gap Analysis — very practical. First, list ALL your requirements. Categorize: MUST HAVE (non-negotiable), SHOULD HAVE (important but can work around), NICE TO HAVE (bonus). Then for each technology option, check: does it meet this requirement or not? If a tool fails even ONE 'Must Have' — it's disqualified! In the example, Tool B fails 'Automated bank feeds' which is a Must Have — disqualified regardless of other features. This prevents you from choosing a tool that LOOKS good but misses something critical. Always start with Must Haves."
Practical Application

The Evaluation Process (Step-by-Step)

  • Step 1: Define requirements (interview stakeholders)
  • Step 2: Research options (shortlist 3-5 candidates)
  • Step 3: Fit-Gap analysis (eliminate non-fits)
  • Step 4: Weighted scoring (rank remaining options)
  • Step 5: TCO analysis (true cost comparison)
  • Step 6: Pilot/demo (test before committing)
  • Step 7: Final decision + implementation plan
SCRIPT: "Here's the complete evaluation process, step by step. Step 1: Define requirements — talk to users, managers, IT. What do they need? Step 2: Research — Google, reviews, ask peers. Shortlist 3-5 viable options. Step 3: Fit-Gap — eliminate any that fail Must Haves. Step 4: Weighted Scoring — rank what's left objectively. Step 5: TCO — calculate true costs for top 2-3. Step 6: PILOT — never buy without testing! Request free trial, demo, proof of concept. Step 7: Decide and plan implementation. This process takes 2-4 weeks for simple tools, 2-6 months for enterprise systems. But it saves you from expensive mistakes."

Professional Brief

Writing a Technology Evaluation Brief

Structure (1-2 pages)

1. Executive Summary (1 paragraph)
2. Business Context (the problem/need)
3. Options Evaluated (what you compared)
4. Evaluation Criteria & Weights
5. Scoring Results (table)
6. Recommendation (your pick + why)
7. Implementation Notes (next steps)

Tips for Professional Briefs

• Be objective — show data, not opinions
• Acknowledge trade-offs honestly
• Include cost projections (TCO)
• Mention risks and mitigations
• Keep it concise — decision makers are busy
• Use tables and visuals for easy scanning
• Cite your sources (vendor websites, reviews)

SCRIPT: "In your career, you'll write Technology Evaluation Briefs — professional documents recommending technology to management. Structure: Executive Summary (one paragraph TL;DR), Business Context (what problem are we solving?), Options (what did you compare?), Criteria with weights, Scoring table, Recommendation (your pick with justification), Implementation notes. Tips: be objective — data not opinions. Acknowledge trade-offs — no tool is perfect. Include TCO. Keep it concise — executives don't read 20 pages. This skill will serve you throughout your career. Your upcoming Technology Brief assignment will follow this structure."
Vendor Evaluation

Evaluating the VENDOR (Not Just the Product)

  • Financial stability: Is the company profitable? Funded? Risk of shutdown?
  • Track record: How long in business? Customer reviews? Case studies?
  • Support quality: Response time? Local support? Training provided?
  • Roadmap: Are they investing in development? What features are coming?
  • Security practices: SOC 2 certified? Data encryption? Compliance?
  • Exit strategy: Can you export your data easily? What if you want to switch?
SCRIPT: "Don't just evaluate the PRODUCT — evaluate the VENDOR. Why? Because you're entering a RELATIONSHIP. Financial stability — if the vendor shuts down, your data might be gone. Track record — how long have they been around? What do customers say? Support — when something breaks at 2am during tax season, will someone answer? Roadmap — are they innovating or stagnant? Security — SOC 2 certification, encryption, compliance? Exit strategy — if you want to leave someday, can you export your data easily? Some vendors trap you (vendor lock-in). Always have an exit plan. These questions are especially important for accounting firms handling YEARS of client financial data."
Summary

Session Takeaways

  • 1. Weighted Scoring Model: define criteria, assign weights, score objectively, compare totals
  • 2. TCO: purchase price is only 20-30% of true cost — include setup, ongoing, and hidden costs

  • 4. Always evaluate BOTH the product AND the vendor (stability, support, exit strategy)
  • 5. Technology Evaluation Briefs: professional documents for recommending technology decisions

Answer Key

Quiz Answers

  • 1. WSM: define criteria, assign % weights, score each option 1-5, multiply score × weight, sum totals, highest wins.
    Rationale: Provides objective, quantifiable comparison instead of gut feelings
  • 2. TCO = all costs over time. Hidden costs: implementation, training, customization, productivity loss, exit/migration costs, ongoing support.
    Rationale: Prevents choosing "cheap" options that cost more over time
  • 3. Must Have, Should Have, Nice to Have. If a tool fails ANY Must Have → immediately disqualified.
    Rationale: Non-negotiable requirements filter out unsuitable options early
  • 4. Any 3: Financial stability, track record, support quality, product roadmap, security practices, exit strategy.
    Rationale: You're entering a long-term relationship, not just buying a product
  • 5. Tool A: needs add-ons (₱500 extra), more manual work (time cost), future migration cost when you outgrow it. Tool B: all-inclusive, saves time (staff efficiency), no migration needed = lower TCO over 3 years.
    Rationale: Demonstrates understanding that "cheap now" often means "expensive later"
